Week 12 Lab — Security Governance

CS 55D: Security in Amazon Web Services  ·  Foothill College  ·  100 pts

Objective: Design the governance layer for a multi-account, multi-region Photo Viewer deployment. Write Service Control Policies (SCPs) that enforce organizational guardrails, understand why they do not apply to the management account, and complete a capstone review of the entire application.

What changes this week: No application code changes. This lab is a policy writing and architecture comprehension exercise. You will write real SCP JSON policies and attach them to OUs — but because your lab account is the management account (exempt from SCPs), and there are no member accounts in the OUs, the SCPs are not enforced on anything. That limitation is the lesson.

Your account’s role: In the Identity Center lab, your account became the management account and was moved into the Production OU. But the management account is exempt from SCPs — placing it inside an OU does not make it governed. In production, a dedicated management account would sit above the OUs running zero workloads. The Photo Viewer would run in a member account — where SCPs actually apply.

Part 1 Design the governance layer — write SCPs

You will write four SCPs that enforce organizational guardrails on member accounts. Three are baselines attached at Root; one is a region lock attached to the Production OU. Writing SCP JSON is the same skill as writing IAM policies — understanding the syntax, NotAction for global services, and condition keys for region restrictions.

Step 1Move your account into the Production OU

Go to AWS Organizations → AWS accounts. Select your account, then click Actions → Move and choose the Production OU.

It may already be there. You moved your account into the Production OU back in the Week 9 Identity Center lab, so it is likely already in place — the pre-flight script confirms this. If you see your account listed under Production, you are done and can skip ahead. Moving it does not change how SCPs affect your account (the management account is always exempt, no matter where it sits), but it keeps the lab's narrative and the region-lock attachment target consistent with what you see in the console.

Step 2Enable SCPs in Organizations

Go to AWS Organizations → Policies → Service control policies. If SCPs are not enabled, click Enable service control policies.

Step 3Write SCP 1 — Deny CloudTrail deletion (baseline)

This policy would be attached at the root of the organization, applying to every member account. No member account should ever be able to disable audit logging.

  • Policy name: deny-cloudtrail-disable
  • Description: Baseline — prevents stopping or deleting CloudTrail trails in any member account
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyCloudTrailDisable",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": [
        "cloudtrail:StopLogging",
        "cloudtrail:DeleteTrail"
      ],
      "Resource": "*"
    }
  ]
}
SCP deny-cloudtrail-disable created in the Organizations console
Step 4Write SCP 2 — Deny public S3 (baseline)

Another baseline policy for all member accounts. This prevents anyone from disabling the S3 Block Public Access settings.

  • Policy name: deny-public-s3
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyDisablingPublicAccessBlock",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "s3:PutBucketPublicAccessBlock",
      "Resource": "*"
    },
    {
      "Sid": "DenyPublicObjectACLs",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": "s3:PutObjectAcl",
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringEquals": {
          "s3:x-amz-acl": ["public-read", "public-read-write"]
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

Two statements, two protections: The first prevents anyone from changing the Block Public Access settings on any bucket. The second prevents setting public ACLs on individual objects. Together, they ensure no S3 resource can be made publicly accessible.

SCP deny-public-s3 created
Step 5Write SCP 3 — Region lock (Production OU)

This policy locks the Production OU to us-east-1 — the region where the Photo Viewer runs. Every member account under this OU would be restricted to that single region. Data sovereignty is enforced at the organizational level: no principal in any production account can create resources in another region, even accidentally.

  • Policy name: restrict-region-us-east-1
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyNonUsEast1",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "NotAction": [
        "iam:*",
        "sts:*",
        "organizations:*",
        "support:*"
      ],
      "Resource": "*",
      "Condition": {
        "StringNotEquals": {
          "aws:RequestedRegion": "us-east-1"
        }
      }
    }
  ]
}

Why NotAction? IAM, STS, Organizations, and Support are global services — they do not operate in a specific region. Denying them with a region condition would break basic account functionality like creating roles or assuming cross-account permissions.

A warning before you ever reuse this pattern: never attach a region-lock SCP to an account you operate in unless that account actually runs in the locked region. In this lab it is safe — you attach it to the Production OU and your account is the exempt management account. But the lecture's example locks to ap-southeast-1; applying that pattern to a real member account running in us-east-1 would lock you out of nearly every service.

SCP restrict-region-us-east-1 created
Step 6Write SCP 4 — Deny Identity Center administration (all member accounts)

Identity Center lives exclusively in the management account. No member account should be able to create its own Identity Center instance, permission sets, or account assignments. All employee access is centralized.

  • Policy name: deny-identity-center-admin
{
  "Version": "2012-10-17",
  "Statement": [
    {
      "Sid": "DenyIdentityCenterAdmin",
      "Effect": "Deny",
      "Action": [
        "sso:CreateInstance",
        "sso:DeleteInstance",
        "sso-admin:CreatePermissionSet",
        "sso-admin:DeletePermissionSet",
        "sso-admin:CreateAccountAssignment"
      ],
      "Resource": "*"
    }
  ]
}

Even if an administrator in a member account has AdministratorAccess, they cannot set up local identity management. Employee access flows only through the centralized Identity Center in the management account.

SCP deny-identity-center-admin created
Step 7Attach SCPs to OUs

Go to AWS Organizations → Organize accounts. Attach the policies as follows:

How attachment scope works: An SCP applies to the node you attach it to and every account beneath it — attachment inherits down the tree. Attaching at Root applies a policy to every account in the organization; attaching to an OU applies it only to accounts in that OU. That is why the three baselines go on Root (they should hold for every member account, including ones added later) while the region lock goes on the Production OU (only production runs in us-east-1). The one account this never reaches is the management account — it is exempt from all SCPs regardless of where they are attached (Root, OU, or directly), so it is never restricted by them.

SCPAttach toIn production, this would govern
deny-cloudtrail-disableRootAll member accounts
deny-public-s3RootAll member accounts
restrict-region-us-east-1Production OUAll member accounts running Photo Viewer workloads
deny-identity-center-adminRootAll member accounts

Note: The Production OU contains your management account (moved there in the Identity Center lab). But the management account is exempt from SCPs — so these policies have no effect on it. The baseline SCPs attached to Root also govern nothing because there are no member accounts. The policies are real; the member accounts they would protect do not yet exist.

Part 2 The management account exemption

You wrote four policies that would prevent CloudTrail deletion, public S3, off-region resources, and local identity management in any member account. Now test whether they apply to your account.

Step 8Try to stop CloudTrail

Go to CloudTrail → Trails. You will see your trail listed. Click on the trail name (the blue hyperlink) to open the trail detail page. On the detail page, click Stop logging in the top-right area, then confirm.

Do not delete the trail. You need to click into the trail to find the stop logging toggle. Do not click the delete button from the trails list. Stopping logging is reversible with one click; deleting the trail requires recreating it.

The action succeeds.

Why? Your account is the management account. SCPs do not apply to the management account — by design. If they did, an overly broad SCP could lock out the entire organization with no way to recover.

This is the single most important lesson about SCPs: they create a ceiling for member accounts only. The management account operates above that ceiling. This is why it must run zero workloads — anything running here has no SCP protection.

In production, the Photo Viewer would run in a member account inside the NA or Asia OU. The deny-cloudtrail-disable SCP would block this action. An attacker who compromised the member account could not disable audit logging, even with AdministratorAccess, even as root.

Re-enable CloudTrail logging immediately — on the same trail detail page, click Start logging.

✓ Verify

CloudTrail stop logging succeeded despite the SCP. You understand this is because the management account is exempt. You re-enabled logging.

CloudTrail stop logging succeeding — demonstrating the management account exemption
Part 3 Production hardening review — capstone

Over twelve weeks, the Photo Viewer was built with deliberate simplifications — choices made for pedagogical clarity that would not be acceptable in production. The production hardening guide on the PhotoViewer page documents each simplification.

Step 9Review the production hardening accordion

Go to the PhotoViewer page. Open the Production Hardening Guide accordion. Read through the items organized by week.

Step 10Select your top three priorities

From the production hardening guide, select the three simplifications you would prioritize first for production deployment. For each one, write:

  • Which simplification you selected (reference it by week and title)
  • Why you ranked it as a top priority (what is the risk if it remains simplified?)
  • What you would need to implement the production alternative (time, cost, complexity)

There is no single correct answer — reasonable priorities depend on the threat model, user base, and compliance requirements. The quality of your reasoning matters more than which items you choose.

Reflection Questions

Question 1

Explain the difference between an IAM deny policy and an SCP deny policy. If both say "Effect": "Deny" on cloudtrail:StopLogging, what makes them fundamentally different? Under what circumstances would each one fail to prevent the action?

Question 2

The Photo Viewer is expanding to Asia. A junior developer argues: “We can save money by running the Asia deployment in the same account as the US deployment, just in a different region. We’ll use IAM policies to keep the teams separate.” Write a response explaining why you agree or disagree, with specific reference to data sovereignty requirements and the management account exemption.
